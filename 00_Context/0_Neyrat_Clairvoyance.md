## Anahtar Özet (TR)

Neyrat, Foucault'nun disiplin toplumları (geçmişe dönük, kurumla şekillendirme) ve Deleuze'ün kontrol toplumları (şimdiyi gerçek zamanlı düzenleme) ayrımına üçüncü bir aşama ekler: **kahinlik toplumları (clairvoyance societies)**. Bu toplumlar geleceğe yönelmiş görünür ama asıl amaçları veri madenciliği ve senaryo planlama yoluyla geleceği öngörülemezlikten çıkarıp şimdiye donduran bir "düz kronoloji" (flat chronology) üretmektir; niyet ile eylem arasındaki boşluğu kapatarak geleceği "ölü" hale getirirler.

Buna karşı Neyrat, Schelling'den hareketle **akronia (achronia)** kavramını geliştirir: zamanın var olabilmesi için zaman-dışı bir kısılmaya (contraction) ihtiyaç duyduğu ontolojik tez. Kahinlik toplumlarının sapkınlığı, akroniayı donuk senaryolar üretmek için kullanmasıdır; oysa akronia canlı bir kaynak olarak kullanılırsa geleceği yeniden açabilir. Çözüm önerisi "geleceği senaryodan çıkarmak" (unscripting): geleceği karartmak (istenmeyen geleceği reddetmek) ama bunu karşı-senaryo değil, arzu edilen bir vaat olarak kurmak. İki tuzaktan kaçınmak gerekir: zamanın senaryolarla aşırı-işgali ve negatif mesiyanizmin (Derrida çizgisi) getirdiği toplam senaryosuzluk.

**Somut örnekler:** telekom şirketlerinin abone kaybını önceden tahmin etmesi, önleyici polislik (Minority Report), iklim senaryo planlaması (IPCC), jeo-mühendislik.

**Projeyle bağlantı:** LLM'lerin niyet/davranış tahmini, öneri sistemleri ve "sonraki adımı öngörme" mantığı, kahinlik toplumu çerçevesinin güncel bir teknik altyapısı olarak okunabilir. Bu metin, LLM'lerle değişen tahakküm siyaseti tartışmasına "öngörü = kontrol biçimi" ve "geleceği açık tutma" karşı-stratejisi ekseninde kaynaklık edecek.

## Key Summary (EN)

Neyrat adds a third stage to Foucault's disciplinary societies (past-oriented, institutional molding) and Deleuze's control societies (real-time modulation of the present): **clairvoyance societies**. These appear future-oriented but actually abolish the future as something unpredictable, using data mining and scenario planning to collapse intention and action into a "flat chronology" — a frozen, saturated present masquerading as the future.

Against this, drawing on Schelling, Neyrat develops **achronia**: the ontological claim that time requires a non-temporal contraction (restriction) to exist at all. Clairvoyance societies pervert achronia by using it to freeze time into fixed scenarios rather than as a living source of openness. His proposed response is "unscripting the future": darkening the future (negating the futures we reject) not as a counter-scenario but as the ground for a genuine political promise. Two traps must be avoided: the over-occupation of time by scenarios, and the negative messianism that refuses all images of the future.

**Concrete cases discussed:** telecom churn prediction, predictive policing (Minority Report), IPCC climate scenario planning, geo-engineering.

**Relevance to this project:** LLM-driven intent/behavior prediction, recommendation systems, and "anticipate-the-next-move" logics can be read as a present-day technical infrastructure for clairvoyance-society control. This text is the reference point for the thought line on how LLMs reshape the politics of domination (tahakküm) — specifically the axis "prediction as a form of control" versus "keeping the future open" as counter-strategy.

## Metin

Let's imagine someone, a stranger, coming from nowhere, from a shapeless future or from an immemorial past, from yesterday or from tomorrow. Deeply melancholic, intensely optimistic, he says:

The future has collapsed, and we live in its ruins. If we do not know yet that the future vanished, it's because these ruins are the materials used to build the virtual realities and the real virtualities that replaced the future with frozen scenarios. To break the flat chronology of preordained futures, it is necessary to unscript the future, to disoccupy it, to disable the immune technologies we created to protect us from the unpredictable nature of temporality. Then and then only, the immemorial machine that projects desirable futures may restore breath and life to our bloodless, blind societies. Then, and then only, we will be able to occupy the future without killing time.

How can we be sure that this call for an unscripting of the future, for a rejection of any kind of scenario concerning the future is not a mere scenario amongst other scenarios, an additional image in the overloaded catalogue of our frozen anticipations? In other words, how is it possible to imagine a different future, an alternative future, without impeding it with our imagination? To answer these questions, we need first to understand that we neither live in the old disciplinary societies à la Michel Foucault, constantly seeking to redo the past, nor in control societies, that could be identified — thanks to Gilles Deleuze — as societies seeking to control the present in real time: nowadays, we live, as I want to argue, in anticipation societies that paradoxically aim to ward off the future. In the first part of this text, I will thus trace the genealogy of these computer-based preventive societies that I call clairvoyance societies. Clairvoyance is the imaginary faculty of discerning objects not present to the senses, or the ability to perceive matters beyond the range of ordinary perception. Clairvoyance societies are grounded on the belief that technology *(s. 80)* can turn this fantasy into a reality. With Arthur C. Clarke, they try to confirm, that "any sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic."[1] In this delusional, but performative, social frame, clairvoyance is the technologically assisted black magic used to turn the future into an obedient beast — an animal so obedient that it looks as if it were dead. I will show how the scenario planning technique, a contemporary risk management tool used by big companies, international institutions, and states, puts the finishing touches to the death of the future. The scenario-making of the future leads to a kind of flat chronology that ultimately gets rid of the future: too occupied, saturated with images, the future tends toward its disappearance.

Somehow, the paradoxical goal of clairvoyance societies is to get rid of temporality, that is to say, to produce a non-time. But the abolition of time in time — that is to say the foreclusion [die Verwerfung] of the future, the erasure of the past, and the avoidance of the present — is the worst dream that humankind could conceive of; a dream of living death, a dream of disappearance within one's lifetime. However, I would like to show that the devastating power that denies temporality, which is at work in clairvoyance societies, is also itself the source of a peculiar temporality. I call this paradoxical power that lies both at the origin and at the end of time achronia. The goal of the second part of my text is to show that achronia tells us something about what we could literally call the night of time: the original and persistent darkness from which time emerged. Achronia is a dark and dangerous power that requires to be named, symbolised, imagined, artistically metabolised, politically instituted, transformed by the ways of living, and recognised in love. Without such operations, achronia turns into a devastating melancholy, or an immunological state of over-protection. In art, love, and life, time opens up only if something closes within time. The opening of time involves the closure of non-time: Chronos requires Achronos. In this sense, the occupation of time, as opposed to the screenwriting at stake in the production of the contemporary flat chronology, must be able to metabolise the dark power of the non-time. Only this metabolisation will enable to project ourselves in the future — again.

### Birth of clairvoyance societies

#### From discipline to control

If we follow the periodisation proposed by Deleuze in his famous 1990 paper, *Postscript on Control Societies*, we successively passed from sovereign societies to disciplinary societies, then from disciplinary societies to control societies. Formed in the 18th and 19th centuries, with a peak — according to Deleuze — "at the beginning *(s. 81)* of the 20th century," disciplinary societies were based on "sites of confinement" whose model was the prison.[2] "In disciplinary societies," Deleuze wrote, "you were always starting all over again (as you went from school to barracks, from barracks to factory)."[3] Indeed, disciplines first and foremost concerned the spheres of the past. If, as Deleuze writes, we keep "starting over" in disciplinary societies, it's because the goal of discipline was to remake human bodies, their habits and their behaviours. Badly built, under-educated, poorly treated, misshapen by their family history, deviant bodies had to be redone by school, corrected by the prison, disinfected by the hospital — that is to say, straightened out by the disciplinary institutions. Only the knowledge of the past, in other words the bio-graphical knowledge, enabled the consideration that this prisoner, this patient, or this schoolboy, once straightened out, might have a different future.

If disciplinary societies related to the past, control societies seek to control the present, anytime and anywhere, out of the narrow interiority of the disciplinary institutions. If discipline can be described as — I quote Deleuze again — "molds, different moldings," control is more a "modulation" without boundaries, "like a self-transmuting molding continually changing from one moment to the next, or like a sieve whose mesh varies from one point to another." The main feature of control societies, Deleuze argues, is that "in control societies you never finish anything."[4] In this sort of society, we must learn all the time, we are trapped in an ongoing training, we must pay attention to the sensitive economy of the Internet, and we suffer from the impossibility to escape the hellish logics of credit and debt.[5] If control societies have no past and no future, it is because they try to create an eternal present. It is the present of the "liquid life" that meta-sociologist Zygmunt Bauman characterised in several of his books,[6] a life subjected to the neoliberal imperative, an imperative that can be summarised as follows: a. Everything is uncertain; thus b. You must be resilient, you must adapt, you must be ready to change your life completely every day, even every hour if necessary.[7] In such liquid societies, individuals became "modulations."

In his 1990 paper, Deleuze related control societies to information systems and computers. Indeed, computers and software are advanced tools to trace individuals, and to monitor them of course. But this kind of surveillance has nothing to do with the Panopticon described by Foucault in *Discipline and Punish: The Birth of the *(s. 82)* Prison*.[8] Imagined by Jeremy Bentham in the 18th century, this prison architecture places the prison guard, invisible, at the centre of a tower. This central position enables him to see each prisoner in his cell without being seen himself. But today, monitoring does not any longer require an enclosed space and a watchtower: one just needs to put an electronic bracelet on the foot of a prisoner. Or to buy a mobile phone, which is first and foremost a connective machine, a talkative machine that expresses our ideas and choices even when we would prefer this snitch to be silent, or at least reserved. What matters in control societies are connections, the automatic character of connections. In control societies, controlling means to be able to process the information from the connections that an individual performs, from a bank withdrawal to internet shopping through all forms of communication. In contrast to surveillance (monitoring), control is a. Access to a machine fed by and fuelling information; b. The recording of this access; and c. The possibility to link all these connections and to visualise the life trajectory of an individual, his bio-line or her bio-trajectory.

To control is not to watch and to monitor from above, it is a way to connect individuals to cognitive mediations and to purchase ever-changing equipment for that cause. The neo-liberal imperative is the socio-ideological function that enables liquefying the values so that nothing can slow down or prevent the connections. Control is indeed the best way to regulate, in real-time, what happens. This regulation is increasingly done by machine-machine communications that constitute the so-called 'Internet of Things' and outnumbers machine-human or human-human communications. In control societies, Deleuze writes, "individuals become 'dividuals' and masses become samples, data, markets or 'banks'."[9] The dividual is the individual who became interchangeable with digital data that are supposed to express its flexible essence, its fluid identity. In such a world of computer regulations, the past is not worth worrying about, as it melts into the form of digital data in a time without discontinuity. The problem of control societies is not to redo the past, but to undo it and to reduce it to usable real-time information.

Such a reservoir of digital data ends up giving timely ideas to engineers, salespersons, governments, and police chiefs: If we can trace the trajectory of a subject, if we can come upon him at the time of his action, why not find out what this subject is close to doing, why not try to control her/him in advance?

#### From control to clairvoyance

To control — and the kind of power implied in control — means to master the connections, and thus to be able to trace the individual, or rather to trace the dividual, that is to say the individual divided into each of his/her actions: to see, to buy, to move, etc. Each action can result in information, which can be grabbed by all the chatty objects that are abundant in our daily lives, from the mobile phone to the surveillance camera through the bank card and all the miniaturised recording devices, like radio-frequency readers (RFID chip). In the computing world, ambient intelligence is the expression used to describe the sensitive form of our electronic environment, reacting and — sometimes — responding to the presence of individuals. The communicating objects that form the Internet of Things are less an interface for the users than for the objects themselves. These objects provide the digital matter that will be controlled, the computer data of databases fuelled by the development of biometrics (identity cards, passports). These databases engender what Antoinette Rouvroy and Thomas Berns call "statistical bodies."[10] Constituted by heterogeneous elements (name, age, occupation, origin, physical description, *(s. 83)* tastes, housing places, travel, banking, internet connections, etc.), these digital bodies are magically homogenised to represent the reproducible and predictable behaviour of the dividuals.

Indeed, one of the objectives of ambient intelligence is to react in real time to what happens. This ability for an instant and dynamic analysis of communicating interconnected objects was made possible by the autonomic computing, or the ability of computer systems to direct themselves, to adapt to unpredictable changes and to take decisions autonomously. Here we come to a tipping point, the passage from control societies to clairvoyance societies. Because the ultimate goal of an 'intelligent' electronic environment would be to act and decide before the action of an individual. It is for this purpose that specific data mining software was created, able to collect, to process information, and to produce correlations about expected behaviours. Basically, data mining is the process of discovering new patterns from large data sets involving methods at the intersection of artificial intelligence, machine learning, statistics and database systems. Behavioural analysis software was designed in order to anticipate future purchases of consumers. Some French phone companies already use data mining to find out if a consumer, given her patterns of use and all the data that the company was able to grab, is about to quit her contract. The work of the salespersons will consequently consist in preventing such terminations, and in pre-emptively proposing another sort of contract.

Clairvoyance is firstly an intensification of control, an intensification that, after a tipping point, also entails a change in the nature of control. Deleuze, as we saw, already considered computer and information structures as the key elements able to explain the passage from disciplinary societies to control societies. Yet it is certain that the considerable developments in communications technology, the autonomic computing, the increased storage capacity and data analysis, bring about a qualitative change in the nature of control. The "statistical bodies" are no longer dividuals associated with trajectories, they are more and more multi-dividual and virtual clouds representing probabilities of actions. One of the fundamental goals of clairvoyance societies is to reduce probability to certainty, as crazy as this certainty might be.

We understand that such a preventive option deeply interests those in power and any kind of preventive policing à la *Minority Report*. For the purpose of clairvoyance societies is not, in the end, to monitor or to control acts, but to anticipate them. What the proactive machines are trying to identify is the intention of the subjects. It is as if no discontinuity was separating intentions, the visible signs expressing the intentional depths of the body, and the resulting actions themselves. As if the future act was already preformed in the inception of intent. This preformation, this lack of separation between the intention and the act, is the announced death of the future as something unpredictable. *(s. 84)* In this regard, the last announcement of clairvoyance societies is that there is nothing to announce, but everything to confirm. In clairvoyance societies, future is collapsed into a paradoxical present: the present of an act that will never occur. The next step is so to say that the passage from the capture of the intent to rendering the scenario of possibilities as — literally — unavoidable.

### Flat chronology

Disciplinary societies tried to redo the past thanks to norms coming from the present; control societies get rid of the past and look for a continuous monitoring of the present, a continuous monitoring in which norms are no longer fixed, but flexible, indefinitely flexible, embodying the last avatar of the neo-liberal imperative. Intensified to the limit, control extends from the present to its anticipation, and becomes clairvoyance. By this term, I do not only mean forecasting, but also preventive action. This is where clairvoyance, as a psychopolitical function, becomes meaningful. What is to be prevented is the occurrence of an outside. Clairvoyance societies try above all to immunise themselves against outside(r)s, be they foreigners, strangers, non-standard singularities, or radical political events likely to contest this form of immunisation. This immunisation is preventive: it invests first and foremost in the future. Indeed, it is no longer possible to create a protection against a political threat generating social antibodies or stable borders: the multiple waves of globalisation (the colonial, the capitalist, and the technological) produced a form of viral interconnection accurately theorised by Jean Baudrillard in the last quarter of the 20th century.[11] If global interconnection can be used to generate transnational police files, viral interconnection is the virulence of social phenomena produced by the structure of the interconnection. Consequently, the only way to ensure social control is to invest in the domain of the future by transforming its potential condition into objective, measurable, available facts. Proactive control, that is to say political clairvoyance, entails investing in advance threatening possibilities, terrorist 'malintents,' or the dissident intentions of radical activists.[12]

The last episode of this chronophobia is at work in scenario planning techniques, whose goal is not even to predict the future, but to build plausible scenarios that can be used as risk management tools. What is risk management? An IPCC (Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change) report of 2007 says that risk management is "oriented towards decision-making." As Melinda Cooper argues,

> scenario planning [is] a forecasting technique which attempts to divine not this or that aspect of the future but the multiple future worlds attendant on alternative actions in the present. In its globalizing scope, scenario planning might be characterized as the practical, methodological counterpart to the power relations described by world systems theory.[13]

Scenario planning is the preferred risk-analysis method of dominant *(s. 85)* national institutions (for example those of the United States) and global political institutions, like the IMF (International Monetary Fund) or the IPCC. In an IPCC report of 2007 entitled *New Assessment Methods and the Characterizations of Future Conditions*, we read that a scenario is "a coherent, internally consistent and plausible description of a possible future state of the world."[14] Scenarios are not based on a mathematical probability, but on "the degree of confidence" that they entail. That is why scenarios, I quote, are "not predictions or forecasts but are alternative images without ascribed likelihoods of how the future might unfold."[15]

What does the future become in scenario planning? According to the National Intelligence Council that publishes a scenario-based report every four years:

> Scenarios are plausible alternative views about how the future may develop. They differ from forecasting in that they do not attempt to predict the future based on linear extrapolations of the past. Scenarios do not seek to project the future. Instead, they focus on the identification of discontinuities and how these could potentially develop as a set over time.[16]

Scenario planning is the best method to get rid of the future. Its strategic goal is not to forecast, but to produce a sort of map of possible worlds or, as Melinda Cooper puts it, "a pluriverse of alternative event-contingent worlds."[17] In the same way that contemporary thinkers formulate a flat ontology, that is to say the idea that every existing being must be considered on the same footing, in the same immanent plan, scenario planning produces a flat chronology negating the differences that constitute the temporality. A flat chronology is nothing but the end of time, or at least an attempt to flatten time and to turn it into a set of spatialised images of possible worlds. In this flat world, almost everything is possible, one can imagine many worlds that are, as Melinda Cooper writes, "unrestrained by the principle of non-contradiction,"[18] that is why they can "entertain contingencies that would be mutually exclusive in any one actual world."[19]

Actually, scenario planning does not take into consideration events that would be at odds with the possibility to plan and to produce scenarios! For example, the IPCC report of 2007 about "the Characterizations of Future Conditions" mentions the possibility of "large-scale singularities" as "extreme, sometimes irreversible, changes in the Earth system such as abrupt cessation of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation or melting of ice sheets in Greenland or West Antarctica;" but the authors of the report immediately add that "with few exceptions, such events are not taken into account in socio-economic assessments of climate change."[20] The condition of the possibility of the flat chronology resulting from scenario planning is the rejection of the impossible, that is to say, the rejection of any sort of radical temporal outside.

### Achronia: power of non-time

#### Schelling and the restriction of time

In the second part of my article, I would like to discuss how it would be possible to avert clairvoyance societies, and to avert what they avert, that is to say, to leave some place for what I just called a radical temporal outside. My ontological hypothesis is the following, a very simple thesis in a way, which can be reduced to the formula: time requires non-time; or even more simply: to open, you must first know how to close. It is simple, but it is also a little bit, let us be honest, counterintuitive. If my hypothesis is correct, we should say that the happening of a new time is not possible without a withdrawal of time; or, to put it another way: the opening of the future and the openness to the future interact to produce a non-future. And this implies an idea that I formulate this way: time requires achronia, that is to say, time requires the non-time at the core of time.

I found an approach to achronia in Schelling's *The Ages of the World*. In the third and final version of the book, the version of 1815, Schelling writes: "It is manifest to hardly anyone that actual power lies *(s. 87)* more in delimitation than expansion and that to withdraw oneself has more to do with might than to give oneself."[21] What is this force of restriction? It is, Schelling explains, the power of the no without which there would be no yes; it is the contraction without which there would be no expansion. "Everything begins in darkness,"[22] Schelling writes, by which he means: "The beginning really only lies in the negation."[23] Indeed, growth and expansion are only possible from their opposites, not from a neutral state where there would be neither contraction nor expansion — because nothing could explain what might be a transition from a neutral state to a positive one: a neutral state cannot generate an existence. For Schelling, the "original negation" that exists before the positivity of existence, that is to say the real existence of something, never disappears, the original negation is "still the mother and wet-nurse of the entire world that is visible to us;"[24] the night of the world always remains present.

It is from this ancestral negation, from this contraction that tends to 'restrict' the being, that Schelling explains — without naming it explicitly — the famous formula of Heraclitus: "Nature loves to hide." In a book called *The Veil of Isis*, philosopher Pierre Hadot proposed this explanation: "What causes things to appear tends to make them disappear (i.e., what causes birth tends to cause death)."[25] In other words, every time something appears, the being of this being, of this object or of this living being, is withdrawn. According to Schelling, the secret of nature that "conceals its secrets" is not an objective knowledge, for no objective reality is hidden behind or beyond the world we know. What the originary contraction produces is the non-objectivity of the world, the non-objectivity from which objects can exist and are thinkable, the unconscious from which we can determine what consciousness is, the non-temporality from which any temporality is generated.[26]

Why can this ontological hypothesis about an originary contraction help us to understand temporality? Because thanks to this hypothesis we can understand that time is unthinkable without non-time. Let us try to prove this point:

a. The past is unthinkable without the non-past. That's the meaning of the famous line from William Faulkner: "The past is never dead. It's not even past."[27] The past only exists because it is, in a sense, always present, urgently present, and consequently always to come — in the guise of obsession, repetition, or idealising projection. Non-past is what prevents the past from being entirely past, that is to say, from having exhaustively happened. A psychological trauma is an accurate illustration of this relation between past and non-past: a trauma is a white hole, white because a part of the situation was not experienced by the traumatised subject, a part of the event was not subjectivised, and the memory of the traumatised subject shelters a partially unwritten story — that's why a trauma is out of the memory, and in this sense immemorial.

b. In the same way, the present is impossible without a form of non-present inhabiting it. This becomes apparent, for example, in what Jacques Derrida identified with the concept of "différance" that is nothing but the non-coincidence of the present to itself, in other words the absence. In an entirely different way, the non-present is a feature of what Deleuze called the Aion, that is to say the event "in its impassibility and impenetrability," the "pure" event that "has no present:" "the agonizing aspect of the pure event is that it is always and at the same time something which has just happened and something about to happen."[28]

c. Finally, future would not be possible without concealing its opposite, that is to say the non-future. We could say that the non-future is what has already happened, that is to say the past; but we know that the past, in a way, is never dead, we know that the past is the living death. We could say that the non-future is just an extension of the present, i.e. a seed just waiting to germinate; but absence is at the core of the present. In this regard, if the event is, as Deleuze says, "something about to happen," the pure event is first and foremost the eternal non-event, that is to say something that will never happen, the fundamental and eternal negation of the future. The non-future is the future as non-happening: the originary contraction of the future. However, without this contraction, there will be no future! Restricted, the future can happen, even partially; but non-restricted, the future would never happen, it would be an absolute non-happening — not a partial one, but an absolute one. This is the power of achronia: non-time at the core of time, achronia opens the temporality. But what would happen if achronia became the goal of our societies?

#### Unscripting the future (darkening and projecting)

Now, let us return to the study of clairvoyance societies: what is their relation with time and the non-time? How do clairvoyance societies cope with achronia? One might think that clairvoyance societies, that are anticipation societies, are future-oriented; but I tried to show that anticipation, in these societies, aims to avert the future, more precisely to eliminate the subversive part of the future. I also wanted to show that scenario planning leads to a flat chronology that turns to be a form of immunisation against the future. To go further, we must understand how it could be possible to stop and maybe to reverse the lethal process of immunisation against the future. A false solution would be to say: to escape flat chronology, we need another scenario, a counter-scenario. A counter-scenario would do nothing more than confirm the scenario planning method and flattening time a little bit more that it already is! Therefore, how can we get out of this temporal trap? The first step should be to counteract the scenario process as such, which is to say to unscenarise, to unscript the future.

First, we must understand that the flat chronology of clairvoyance societies entails a motionless time, a kind of cryogenically frozen time, a certain type of non-time. This is the perversion of clairvoyance societies: instead of using the non-time to open time, they use time to produce the non-time, the non-time of scripted futures. The achronic power is no longer the power of original restriction. Achronia is fixed in the form of an immunised temporality, a flat chronology where time is blocked in the form of scenarios. To prevent this impediment, to warm up and liquefy the cryogenically frozen time of scenarios, it seems first and foremost necessary to get rid of clairvoyance societies' inherent perversion (to produce a frozen non-time instead of using non-time as a living source) and to put the non-time back to its original place. As Schelling noted: "Does not every existence presuppose contraction, precisely because it is existence? Is there any kind of existence that was not an existence first in involution? Is there any kind of free life that was not delivered from a negate state?"[29]

To put the non-time back to its original place can open an access to the dimension of the future. Indeed, it is necessary to remember that the future is rooted in darkness, in the non-knowing, in the unanticipatable, in what cannot happen, or what can never completely happen, like the Deleuzian event: an impenetrable, irremediably non-present. But Schelling enables us to understand that this night and this non-knowing are the effect of a contraction, a restriction. This means that the future will always be the negation of a non-future that precedes the future. Thus, the maxim of a real future-oriented ethic could be formulated like this: "Before choosing the future, consider the negation from which this future might emerge." Let's give some illustrations. A society that decides to secure every aspect of life should know that a total securisation would require the murder of freedom. A society that tends more and more to consider geo-engineering as *(s. 89)* an essential technology to fight against climate change, denies the possibility of a society that will not be based on extraction of energy and the "choice of fire" of our thermo-industrial civilisation, to quote the words of historian Alain Gras:[30] We decided to develop coal mining in the 19th century, we decided to promote fossil fuels, it was a choice and certainly not the effect of any inexorable and anonymous 'progress.' These decisions could have been different from what they actually were. The scripted time of scenario planning flattens the future, making equivalent and non-contradictory several likely options.

Unscripting the future would therefore mean to introduce a principle of non-equivalence capable of breaking the flat chronology. This principle of non-equivalence requires that we ask ourselves: "What future do we favour? What future do we want to project?" It is certain that the projection machine of human societies has stalled for the last fifty years. Firstly, because of the ideological collapse of the great narratives of emancipation as analysed by Jean-François Lyotard;[31] secondly because of the ecological collapse,[32] that is to say the fact that nowadays, ecological disasters, such as climate change, are no longer considered as events to come, but as daily observations. Does this mean that from now on we cannot any longer project ourselves into the future? Does it mean that we cannot project a future? In a way, scenario planning is the pure confirmation of such impossibility. Scenario planning is the best way to bury the future after its collapse. Hence it seems to be a tempting, even if ultimately misleading strategy to get rid of flat chronology to burn its scenery and thus to destroy the idols that impede the future. Yet this purely iconoclastic gesture would be yet another kind of fetishism, not the fetishism of scenario-planned futures and their frozen images, but the fetishism of the total lack of scenarios and expectations, the negative fetishism of pure darkness. Particularly prized by many thinkers of the second part of the 20th century, like Jacques Derrida, the fetishism of nothingness resulted in an outright negative messianism whose theoretical core could be described as follows: a. Any image that anticipates the future is bad because it denies the unpredictability of time. b. Therefore, it is essential to leave time to its darkness, to welcome — without images, and without names — the unforeseen events that could interrupt the continuity of time.

But I argue that the darkening of the time is itself a condition of the possibility of temporal projection, and that it does not constitute a rejection of any sort of projection. Darkness is the effect of a negative operation; this negation creates a dialectic tension between the future we want and the opposite of this future.

So, let's sum up: a. If we want to unscript the future, we must darken it. b. Darkening the future is an ontological and political operation thanks to which we can negate the futures that we reject. c. The projection of the future must be raised against what we do not want in order to promise what we want.

### Conclusion: the wild wind of tomorrow

In the era of digital capitalism, the future is abandoned to scenarios. Getting rid of the thought that generates such a flat chronology must however not lead to the negative messianism for which the opening of time requires strictly not to shape the way that such openness could initiate. I argue that it is not possible to overcome the ideological and ecological collapse of time we are exposed to at present without giving back flesh to temporality, without giving back to this temporality a figure of revolutionary aspirations through which political reforms are conceivable: a political reform without a revolutionary background to support it must be called a mere adaptation, or, in polite terms, resilience. Radical politics should nowadays propose something else than a mere alternative scenario. Indeed, we know that two pitfalls must be avoided: The over-occupation of time with anticipations and scenarios and the disoccupation of time on behalf of a negative messianism.

Between the two, radical politics have to draw the promise of a more just world, a world that will not be immunised against outside(r)s. In this regard, let us remember that a promise is not an anticipation, for a promise does not foresee the future. A promise is a metaphorical support thanks to which we can desire, from now, the wild wind of tomorrow.
